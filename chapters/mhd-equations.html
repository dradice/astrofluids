22  The MHD Equations

22.1 Lorentz Force

These notes present a concise derivation of the equations of magnetohydrodynamics (MHD) and a discussion of the underlying assumptions. We consider a quasi-neutral gas of ions and electrons immersed in a magnetic field. Let \(\mathbf{w}_e\) and \(\mathbf{w}_i\) be the drift velocities of electrons and ions, respectively. The Lorentz force experienced by each electron is \[ \mathbf{F}_e = - e \mathbf{w}_e \times \mathbf{B}. \tag{22.1}\] The force experienced by the ions is \[ \mathbf{F}_i = Ze \mathbf{w}_i \times \mathbf{B}. \tag{22.2}\] So the total force per unit volume is \[ \mathbf{f}= n_e \mathbf{F}_e + n_i \mathbf{F}_i = e (n_i Z\mathbf{w}_i - n_e \mathbf{w}_e) \times \mathbf{B} =: \mathbf{j} \times \mathbf{B}. \tag{22.3}\] Here \(\mathbf{j}\) is the electric current density, measured in \(\mathrm{A\,m^{-2}}\). Note that we have not included the electric force. The electric force per unit volume is \(q \mathbf{E}\), where \(q\) is the charge density, and it is negligible because the plasma is quasi-neutral, \(q \approx 0\) (Chapter 21).

The equations of MHD are just the “usual” hydrodynamics equations with the addition of the Lorentz force on the RHS: \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\mathrm{d} \rho}{\mathrm{d} t} &= - \rho \nabla \cdot \mathbf{v}, \\ \rho \frac{\mathrm{d} \mathbf{v}}{\mathrm{d} t} &= \boxed{\mathbf{j} \times \mathbf{B}} - \nabla p, \\ \rho \frac{\mathrm{d} \varepsilon}{\mathrm{d} t} &= - p \nabla \cdot \mathbf{v}. \end{aligned} \tag{22.4}\] These equations look deceptively simple. The real complication is that \(\mathbf{j}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) are not prescribed, but are self-consistently produced by the fluid. We have also implicitly made the following assumption:

Assumption 22.1 (Negligible drift momentum) The drift motion of the charged particles in the plasma that gives rise to the current is sufficiently small that we can neglect its contribution to the macroscopic momentum \(\rho \mathbf{v}\). In other words, in a frame in which \(\mathbf{v}= 0\), we have \(m_i n_i \mathbf{w}_i + m_e n_e \mathbf{w}_e \approx 0\).

This assumption is satisfied in most situations encountered in terrestrial and astrophysical applications, because the currents are sufficiently weak and the charge densities sufficiently large that we can treat the plasma as being a single fluid. This can be violated in very tenuous plasmas, for example in the magnetosphere of dying pulsars, in which the field and spin have decayed to the point that pair production is no longer efficient. In this case, one needs to go beyond magnetohydrodynamics and use the multi-fluid magnetohydrodynamics equations, or employ a fully kinetic treatment. Another situation in which this assumption can fail is if the degree of ionization of the plasma is small, so there is drift between the charged component of the gas and the neutrals, ambipolar diffusion. As we will see in Chapter 23, ambipolar diffusion plays an important role, for example, in star formation.

22.2 Ohm’s Law

We need two more equations, in addition to the equation of state, to close Equation 22.4. A first equation is the equation prescribing how currents \(\mathbf{j}\) arise in the plasma. The simplest model for the current is to assume that it is proportional to the electric field.

Assumption 22.2 (Ohm’s law) The electric current in the plasma \(\mathbf{j}\) is proportional to the electric field \(\mathbf{E}'\) in the frame of the plasma (in which \(\mathbf{v}= 0\)): \(\mathbf{j}= \sigma \mathbf{E}'\).

The quantity \(\sigma\) is called the electrical conductivity, it is the inverse of the electrical resistivity \(1 / \sigma\), which you might be familiar with from electromagnetism. In SI units1 \[ [\mathbf{E}] = \mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}, \qquad [\mathbf{j}] = \mathrm{A\,m^{-2}}, \tag{22.5}\] so the units of electrical conductivity are \[ [\sigma] = \mathrm{A\,V^{-1}\,m^{-1}} = \mathrm{S\,m^{-1}} . \tag{22.6}\] Here \(\mathrm{S} = \mathrm{A\,V^{-1}}\) is the siemens, the inverse of the ohm. Again, note that in SI units the current is an independent quantity, measured in ampere, so the Maxwell and MHD equations contain the constants \(\epsilon_0\) and \(\mu_0\). Be very careful not to mix Gaussian and SI units, because the equations are different in the two cases (Chapter 21).

One subtlety with the new assumption is what we mean for the “electric field in the frame of the plasma”. The hydrodynamics equations are invariant with respect to Galilean transformations, that is, if \(\mathbf{u}\) is a velocity in the Eulerian frame, then in the fluid frame we have \[ \mathbf{u}' =\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}. \tag{22.7}\] On the other hand, Maxwell equations are invariant with respect to Lorentz transformations. As such, Maxwell equations and the equations of non-relativistic hydrodynamics are fundamentally incompatible. To be able to put them together in a consistent way, we need to assume that \(\mathbf{E}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) transform according to Lorentz transformations in the nonrelativistic limit.

Assumption 22.3 (Nonrelativistic limit) We assume \(v \ll c\), \(| \mathbf{E} | \ll c | \mathbf{B} |\), and \(\left| \epsilon_0 \frac{\partial \mathbf{E}}{\partial t} \right| \ll | \mathbf{j} |\). The second two assumptions are needed to ensure that charges are not relativistic and the plasma quasi-neutral.

Under these assumptions, it is possible to show that \[ \mathbf{B}' =\mathbf{B}, \qquad \mathbf{E}' =\mathbf{E}+ \mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B}, \qquad \mathbf{j}' =\mathbf{j}. \tag{22.8}\] Accordingly, Ohm’s law becomes \[ \mathbf{j}' = \sigma \mathbf{E}' \Longrightarrow \mathbf{j}= \sigma \left( \mathbf{E}+ \mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B} \right) . \tag{22.9}\] In Ampère’s law, \[ \nabla \times \mathbf{B}= \mu_0 \mathbf{j}+ \frac{1}{c^2} \frac{\partial \mathbf{E}}{\partial t}, \tag{22.10}\] where we have used \(\mu_0 \epsilon_0 = 1 / c^2\), we can drop the displacement current (Assumption 22.3). This makes sense, because \(E \sim v B\), so if the fields change over a length \(L\) and a time \(T \sim L / v\), the displacement current is of order \(v B / (c^2 T) \sim (v / c)^2 B / L\), while \(\nabla \times \mathbf{B}\) is of order \(B / L\). So we obtain \[ \nabla \times \mathbf{B}= \mu_0 \mathbf{j}. \tag{22.11}\] Then the Lorentz force becomes \[ \boxed{\mathbf{f}=\mathbf{j} \times \mathbf{B}= \frac{1}{\mu_0} (\nabla \times \mathbf{B}) \times \mathbf{B}.} \tag{22.12}\] With this, we have completely eliminated the current from the MHD equations: \[ \boxed{\rho \frac{\mathrm{d} \mathbf{v}}{\mathrm{d} t} = \frac{1}{\mu_0} (\nabla \times \mathbf{B}) \times \mathbf{B}- \nabla p.} \tag{22.13}\] We still need another equation for \(\mathbf{B}\) to completely close the MHD equations.

22.3 Faraday’s Law

The missing equation is given by Faraday’s law, also known as the induction equation: \[ \frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t} = - \nabla \times \mathbf{E}. \tag{22.14}\] Here, we use again Ohm’s law to eliminate \(\mathbf{E}\): \[ \frac{1}{\sigma} \mathbf{j} =\mathbf{E}+ \mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B} \Longrightarrow \mathbf{E}= \frac{1}{\sigma} \mathbf{j}- \mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B} . \tag{22.15}\] We use again Ampère’s law to remove \(\mathbf{j}= \frac{1}{\mu_0} \nabla \times \mathbf{B}\): \[ \mathbf{E}= \frac{1}{\mu_0 \sigma} \nabla \times \mathbf{B}- \mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B} =: \eta \nabla \times \mathbf{B}- \mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B} . \tag{22.16}\] Inserting this back into the induction equation yields: \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t} &= - \nabla \times \mathbf{E} \\ &= \nabla \times (\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B}) - \nabla \times (\eta \nabla \times \mathbf{B}) . \end{aligned} \tag{22.17}\] If \(\eta\) is constant, then \[ \nabla \times (\nabla \times \mathbf{B}) = \nabla (\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B}) - \nabla^2 \mathbf{B}, \tag{22.18}\] so we find \[ \boxed{\frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t} = \nabla \times (\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B}) + \eta \nabla^2 \mathbf{B}.} \tag{22.19}\] The coefficient \(\eta = 1 / (\mu_0 \sigma)\) has units of \(\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}\) and is called the magnetic diffusivity. Note that some texts use \(\eta\) for the resistivity \(1 / \sigma\) instead. The magnetic diffusivity is extremely small in most astrophysical systems.

TipExample

Consider the warm interstellar medium, with \(T \approx 10^4\,\mathrm{K}\). Its magnetic diffusivity is \(\eta \approx 500\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}\) (Equation 23.27). Consider a region of size \(L \sim 1\,\mathrm{pc}\) in which the gas moves with velocity \(v \sim 10\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}\). The ratio between the time it takes for the magnetic field to diffuse over the region, \(L^2 / \eta\), and the time it takes for the gas to cross it, \(L / v\), is \[ \mathrm{R}_M = \frac{L v}{\eta} \sim 6 \times 10^{17} . \tag{22.20}\] This is the magnetic Reynolds number, which we will discuss in Chapter 23. For comparison, copper has a conductivity of about \(6 \times 10^{7}\,\mathrm{S\,m^{-1}}\), so its magnetic diffusivity is \(1 / (\mu_0 \sigma) \approx 1.3 \times 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}\). A copper bar of length \(1\,\mathrm{m}\) moving with velocity \(1\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) has \(\mathrm{R}_M \sim 100\). In other words, resistivity is negligible in astrophysics, but not in the laboratory. Even liquid-metal experiments designed to study the generation of magnetic fields struggle to reach \(\mathrm{R}_M \sim 10^3\).

This is the last equation. The full set of MHD equations is \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\mathrm{d} \rho}{\mathrm{d} t} &= - \rho \nabla \cdot \mathbf{v}, \\ \rho \frac{\mathrm{d} \mathbf{v}}{\mathrm{d} t} &= \frac{1}{\mu_0} (\nabla \times \mathbf{B}) \times \mathbf{B}- \nabla p - \rho \nabla \Psi, \\ \rho \frac{\mathrm{d} \varepsilon}{\mathrm{d} t} &= - p \nabla \cdot \mathbf{v} + \frac{j^2}{\sigma}, \\ \frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t} &= \nabla \times (\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B}) + \eta \nabla^2 \mathbf{B}, \\ \nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} &= 0, \end{aligned} \tag{22.21}\] where \(\mathbf{j} = \nabla \times \mathbf{B} / \mu_0\). The last term in the energy equation, \(j^2 / \sigma = \mu_0 \eta j^2\), describes the heating of the plasma by the electric currents, also known as Joule (or Ohmic) heating. It vanishes in ideal MHD. The last equation, \(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0\), is a constraint on the initial data. If it is satisfied at \(t = 0\), the induction equation guarantees that it is satisfied at all times, because the divergence of a curl vanishes (see the in-class worksheet).

The first three equations can be converted to the Eulerian frame with the usual substitution \[ \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d} t} \longrightarrow \frac{\partial}{\partial t} + (\mathbf{v} \cdot \nabla) . \tag{22.22}\] If \(\eta = 0\), these are known as the ideal-MHD equations. If \(\eta > 0\), these are called the resistive-MHD equations.

22.4 Conservation Laws

It is possible to rearrange the terms of the momentum equation to obtain an equation that shows the conservation of the linear momentum: \[ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} & \frac{\partial (\rho \mathbf{v})}{\partial t} \\ & \quad + \nabla \cdot \biggl[ \rho \mathbf{v} \otimes \mathbf{v}+ \Bigl( p + \frac{B^2}{2 \mu_0} \Bigr) \mathbf{I} \\ & \qquad\qquad - \frac{\mathbf{B} \otimes \mathbf{B}}{\mu_0} \biggr] =\mathbf{f}, \end{aligned}} \tag{22.23}\] where \(\mathbf{f}\) is some external force, e.g., gravity. To derive this equation, we use the vector identity \[ (\nabla \times \mathbf{B}) \times \mathbf{B} = (\mathbf{B} \cdot \nabla) \mathbf{B} - \nabla \left( \frac{B^2}{2} \right) \tag{22.24}\] and the fact that, since \(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0\), \[ \nabla \cdot (\mathbf{B} \otimes \mathbf{B}) = (\mathbf{B} \cdot \nabla) \mathbf{B} + \mathbf{B} (\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B}) = (\mathbf{B} \cdot \nabla) \mathbf{B} . \tag{22.25}\] So the Lorentz force can be written as the divergence of a tensor, \(\frac{1}{\mu_0} (\nabla \times \mathbf{B}) \times \mathbf{B} = - \nabla \cdot \left[ \frac{B^2}{2 \mu_0} \mathbf{I} - \frac{\mathbf{B} \otimes \mathbf{B}}{\mu_0} \right]\), and the rest of the derivation is the same as in Chapter 6. Here, we limit ourselves to discuss a couple of the consequences of this equation. The first is that the magnetic field does not contribute to the momentum \(\rho \mathbf{v}\), consistently with Assumption 22.1. This is not the case in relativistic MHD, in which the inertia of the electromagnetic field is also taken into account. Second, we see that the Lorentz force splits into two components: one that resembles an additional pressure term \(B^2 / 2 \mu_0\), plus a term proportional to \(\mathbf{B} \otimes \mathbf{B}\). We will discuss these terms in more detail in Chapter 24.

The energy conservation equation now takes into account both the gas energy and the energy in the electromagnetic field and can be obtained by combining the gas-dynamics energy equation with the Poynting theorem. For simplicity, we consider ideal MHD (\(\eta = 0\)). The equation describing the energy conservation for the gas (Equation 6.22) is \[ \begin{aligned} & \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left[ \rho \left( \varepsilon + \tfrac{1}{2} v^2 \right) \right] + \nabla \cdot \left[ \left( \rho \varepsilon + \tfrac{1}{2} \rho v^2 + p \right) \mathbf{v} \right] \\ & \qquad =\mathbf{f} \cdot \mathbf{v}+ (\mathbf{j} \times \mathbf{B}) \cdot \mathbf{v} . \end{aligned} \tag{22.26}\] This is just the usual energy equation, with the inclusion of a new term representing the work done by the Lorentz force. The latter can be rewritten using Equation 22.16 as \[ (\mathbf{j} \times \mathbf{B}) \cdot \mathbf{v}= - (\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B}) \cdot \mathbf{j}= \mathbf{E} \cdot \mathbf{j}. \tag{22.27}\] You might recognize this as being the energy sink term in the Poynting theorem (Equation 21.26), which reads \[ \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{EM}}}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot \mathbf{S}= - \mathbf{j} \cdot \mathbf{E}, \tag{22.28}\] where \(u_{\mathrm{EM}}\) is the electromagnetic field energy, which in our case is: \[ u_{\mathrm{EM}} = \frac{\epsilon_0 E^2}{2} + \frac{B^2}{2 \mu_0} \approx \frac{B^2}{2 \mu_0} = u_{\mathrm{B}}, \tag{22.29}\] consistently with our assumption that the dynamics is nonrelativistic. The Poynting vector is, using again Equation 22.16: \[ \mathbf{S}= \frac{1}{\mu_0} (\mathbf{E} \times \mathbf{B}) = \frac{\mathbf{B} \times (\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B})}{\mu_0} . \tag{22.30}\] Combining Equation 22.26 and Equation 22.28, we obtain \[ \begin{aligned} & \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left[ \rho \left( \varepsilon + \tfrac{1}{2} v^2 \right) + u_{\mathrm{B}} \right] \\ & \quad + \nabla \cdot \left[ \left( \rho \varepsilon + \tfrac{1}{2} \rho v^2 + p \right) \mathbf{v}+\mathbf{S} \right] =\mathbf{f} \cdot \mathbf{v}. \end{aligned} \tag{22.31}\] In full form: \[ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} & \partial_t \left[ \rho \left( \varepsilon + \tfrac{1}{2} v^2 \right) + \frac{B^2}{2 \mu_0} \right] \\ & \quad + \nabla \cdot \biggl[ \left( \rho \varepsilon + \tfrac{1}{2} \rho v^2 + p \right) \mathbf{v} \\ & \qquad\qquad + \frac{\mathbf{B} \times (\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B})}{\mu_0} \biggr] =\mathbf{f} \cdot \mathbf{v}. \end{aligned}} \tag{22.32}\] This equation highlights the fact that magnetic and fluid energies are not separately conserved, but their sum is. In other words, it is possible to convert gas kinetic energy into magnetic energy (dynamo effect) or magnetic energy into kinetic and internal energy (magnetic reconnection, Chapter 23). In resistive MHD, Equation 22.32 still holds. In this case, Equation 22.27 has an additional term, \(- j^2 / \sigma\), and the Poynting flux has an additional term proportional to \(\eta\). However, these are exactly compensated by the Joule heating in the energy equation of the gas. In other words, the magnetic energy dissipated by the electric currents is transferred to the internal energy of the gas.

22.5 In-class worksheet

  1. Write the Maxwell equations in SI units.

  2. Write Ampère’s law in the nonrelativistic limit. Use it to eliminate the current in the Lorentz force per unit volume experienced by a magnetized fluid: \[ \mathbf{f}=\mathbf{j} \times \mathbf{B}. \tag{22.33}\]

  3. Write Ohm’s law for an electrically conducting, quasi-neutral fluid. Use it to eliminate the electric field in Faraday’s law.

  4. Show that, if \(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0\) at \(t = 0\), then the induction equation guarantees that \(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0\) at all times.

22.6 Exercises

Exercise 22.1 (Angular momentum of the solar wind)  

  1. Starting from the equations of ideal MHD, show that the angular momentum of the plasma, \[ \mathbf{J} = \int_V \mathbf{r} \times \rho \mathbf{v} \mathrm{d} V, \tag{22.34}\] subject to an external force per unit volume \(\mathbf{f}\), satisfies \[ \frac{\mathrm{d} \mathbf{J}}{\mathrm{d} t} = \int_V \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{f} \mathrm{d} V - \oint_S \left[ (\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathrm{d} \mathbf{S}) (\mathbf{r} \times \rho \mathbf{v}) + (p + B^2 / 2 \mu_0) \mathbf{r} \times \mathrm{d} \mathbf{S}- (\mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{B}) (\mathbf{B} \cdot \mathrm{d} \mathbf{S}) / \mu_0 \right] . \tag{22.35}\] [Hint. Follow the steps of Exercise 6.1.]

  2. Assume that the magnetic field is stationary in a frame rotating with the angular velocity of the Sun. Show that if the solar wind is infinitely conducting, then (in cylindrical coordinates \(R, \phi, z\)) \[ B_{\phi} v_R = (v_{\phi} - \Omega R) B_R, \tag{22.36}\] where \(\Omega\) is the angular velocity of the Sun.

  3. Let \(\dot{J}\) be the \(z\)-component of the torque across a cylinder of radius \(R\) and height \(2 R\). Assume that \(\dot{J}\) does not depend on \(R\), and neglect the flux of angular momentum through the top and bottom of the cylinder. Show that \(\dot{J}\) can be written as \[ - \dot{J} = 2 \pi \int_{- R}^R \left( R \rho v_R v_{\phi} - R \frac{B_{\phi} B_R}{\mu_0} \right) R \mathrm{d} z. \tag{22.37}\] Assume that the expression in parentheses is independent of \(z\), and that \(4 \pi \rho R^2 v_R\), the mass flux of the solar wind through the cylinder, is a constant. Show that \[ - \frac{\dot{J}}{4 \pi \rho R^2 v_R} = Rv_{\phi} \left( 1 - \frac{B_R^2}{\mu_0 \rho v_R^2} \right) + \frac{B_R^2}{\mu_0 \rho v_R^2} \Omega R^2 . \tag{22.38}\]

  4. There is a radius, \(R_A\), called the Alfvén radius where the radial Alfvén speed \(v_A = B_R / \sqrt{\mu_0 \rho}\) is equal to \(v_R\). Evaluate the angular momentum loss per unit mass at this radius and show that it is equal to \(\Omega R_A^2\).

  5. If \(R_A\) is 10 \(R_{\odot}\), approximately what fraction of its mass must the Sun lose to slow its rotation down by a factor of 2? Take the moment of inertia of the Sun to be \(0.06 M_{\odot} R_{\odot}^2\). What fraction of its mass has the Sun lost in 5 billion years if the solar wind was steady and the number density at the Earth is \(10\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}\) and the velocity is \(300\,\mathrm{km/s}\)?
