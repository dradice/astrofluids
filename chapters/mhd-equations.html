23  The MHD Equations

23.1 Lorentz Force

These notes present a concise derivation of the equations of magnetohydrodynamics (MHD) and a discussion of the underlying assumptions. We consider a quasi-neutral gas of ions and electrons immersed in an external magnetic field. Let \(\mathbf{w}_e\) and \(\mathbf{w}_i\) be the drift velocities of electrons and ions, respectively. The Lorentz force experienced by each electron is \[ \mathbf{F}_e = - \frac{e}{c} \mathbf{w}_e \times \mathbf{B}. \tag{23.1}\] The force experienced by the ions is \[ \mathbf{F}_i = \frac{Ze}{c} \mathbf{w}_i \times \mathbf{B}. \tag{23.2}\] So the total force per unit volume is \[ \mathbf{f}= n_e \mathbf{F}_e + n_i \mathbf{F}_i = \frac{e}{c} (n_i Z\mathbf{w}_i - n_e \mathbf{w}_e) \times \mathbf{B} =: \mathbf{j} \times \mathbf{B}. \tag{23.3}\] Note that we have absorbed the \(1 / c\) factor in the definition of \(\mathbf{j}\), following a standard convention in magnetohydrodynamics.

The equations of MHD are just the “usual” hydrodynamics equations with the addition of the Lorentz force on the RHS: \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\mathrm{d} \rho}{\mathrm{d} t} &= - \rho \nabla \cdot \mathbf{v}, \\ \rho \frac{\mathrm{d} \mathbf{v}}{\mathrm{d} t} &= \boxed{\mathbf{j} \times \mathbf{B}} - \nabla p, \\ \rho \frac{\mathrm{d} \varepsilon}{\mathrm{d} t} &= - p \nabla \cdot \mathbf{v}. \end{aligned} \tag{23.4}\] These equations look deceivably simple. The real complication is that \(\mathbf{j}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) are not prescribed, but are self-consistently produced by the fluid. We have also implicitly made the following assumption

Assumption 23.1 (Negligible drift momentum) The drift motion of the charged particles in the plasma that gives rise to the current is sufficiently small that we can neglect its contribution to the macroscopic momentum \(\rho \mathbf{v}\). In other words, in a frame in which \(\mathbf{v}= 0\), we have \(m_i n_i \mathbf{w}_i + m_e n_e \mathbf{w}_e \approx 0\).

This assumption is satisfied in most situations encountered in terrestrial and astrophysical applications, because the currents are sufficiently weak and the charge densities sufficiently large that we can treat the plasma as being a single fluid. This can be violated in very tenuous plasmas, for example in the magnetosphere of dying pulsars, in which the field and spin have decayed to the point that pair production is no longer efficient. In this case, one needs to go beyond magnetohydrodynamics and use the multi-fluid magnetohydrodynamics equations, or employ a fully kinetic treatment. Another situation in which this assumption can fail is if the degree of ionization of the plasma is small, so there is drift between the charged component of the gas and the neutrals, ambipolar diffusion. As we will see later, ambipolar diffusion plays an important role, for example, in stellar formation.

23.2 Ohm’s Law

We need two more equations, in addition to the equation of state, to close Equation 23.4. A first equation is the equation prescribing how currents \(\mathbf{j}\) arise in the plasma. The simplest model for the current is to assume that it is proportional to the electric field.

Assumption 23.2 (Ohm’s law) The electric current in the plasma \(\mathbf{j}\) is proportional to the electric field \(\mathbf{E}'\) in the frame of the plasma (in which \(\mathbf{v}= 0\)): \(\mathbf{j}= \sigma \mathbf{E}'\).

The quantity \(\sigma\) is called the electrical conductivity, it is the inverse of the electrical resistivity \(\eta = 1 / \sigma\), which you might be familiar from electromagnetism. In CGS units1 \[ [\mathbf{E}] = \mathrm{cm}^{- 1 / 2} \mathrm{g}^{1 / 2} \mathrm{s}^{- 1}, \qquad [\mathbf{j}] = \mathrm{cm}^{- 3 / 2} \mathrm{g}^{1 / 2} \mathrm{s}, \tag{23.5}\] so the units of electrical conductivity is \[ [\sigma] = \mathrm{cm}^{- 1} \mathrm{s}^2 . \tag{23.6}\] Again, note that in CGS units all electromagnetic quantities have units that can be expressed in terms of fundamental length, time, and mass. Charge and current are not independent quantities. This means that the Maxwell and MHD equations do not have new fundamental constants. Be very careful not to mix CGS and SI units, because the equations are different in the two cases.

One subtlety with the new assumption is what we mean for the “electric field in the frame of the plasma”. The hydrodynamics equations are invariant with respect to Galilean transformations, that if \(\mathbf{u}\) is a velocity in the Eulerian frame, then in the fluid frame we have \[ \mathbf{u}' =\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}. \tag{23.7}\] On the other hand, Maxwell equations are invariant with respect to Lorentz transformations. As such, Maxwell equations and the equations of non-relativistic hydrodynamics are fundamentally incompatible. To be able to put them together in a consistent way, we need to assume that \(\mathbf{E}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) transform according to Lorentz transformations in the nonrelativistic limit.

Assumption 23.3 (Nonrelativistic limit) We assume \(v \ll c\), \(| \mathbf{E} | \ll | \mathbf{B} |\), and \(\left| \frac{1}{c} \frac{\partial \mathbf{E}}{\partial t} \right| \ll | \mathbf{j} |\). The second two assumptions are needed to ensure that charges are not relativistic and the plasma quasi-neutral.

Under these assumptions, it is possible to show that \[ \mathbf{B}' =\mathbf{B}, \qquad \mathbf{E}' =\mathbf{E}+ \frac{\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B}}{c}, \qquad \mathbf{j}' =\mathbf{j}. \tag{23.8}\] Accordingly, Ohm’s law becomes \[ \mathbf{j}' = \sigma \mathbf{E}' \Longrightarrow \mathbf{j}= \sigma \left( \mathbf{E}+ \frac{\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B}}{c} \right) . \tag{23.9}\] In Ampere’s law2, \[ \nabla \times \mathbf{B}= 4 \pi \mathbf{j}+ \frac{1}{c} \frac{\partial \mathbf{E}}{\partial t}, \tag{23.10}\] we can drop the displacement current to obtain \[ \nabla \times \mathbf{B}= 4 \pi \mathbf{j}. \tag{23.11}\] Then the Lorentz force becomes \[ \boxed{\mathbf{f}=\mathbf{j} \times \mathbf{B}= \frac{1}{4 \pi} (\nabla \times \mathbf{B}) \times \mathbf{B}.} \tag{23.12}\] With this, we have completely eliminated the current from the MHD equations: \[ \boxed{\rho \frac{\mathrm{d} \mathbf{v}}{\mathrm{d} t} = \frac{1}{4 \pi} (\nabla \times \mathbf{B}) \times \mathbf{B}- \nabla p.} \tag{23.13}\] We still need another equation for \(\mathbf{B}\) to completely close the MHD equations.

23.3 Faraday’s Law

The missing equation is given by Faraday’s law, also known as the induction equation: \[ \frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t} = - c \nabla \times \mathbf{E}. \tag{23.14}\] Here, we use again Ohm’s law to eliminate \(\mathbf{E}\): \[ \frac{1}{\sigma} \mathbf{j} =: \eta \mathbf{j}=\mathbf{E}+ \frac{\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B}}{c} \Longrightarrow \mathbf{E}= \eta \mathbf{j}- \frac{\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B}}{c} . \tag{23.15}\] We use again Ampere’s law to remove \(\mathbf{j}= \frac{1}{4 \pi} \nabla \times \mathbf{B}\): \[ \mathbf{E}= \frac{\eta}{4 \pi} \nabla \times \mathbf{B}- \frac{\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B}}{c} . \tag{23.16}\] Inserting this back into the induction equation yields: \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t} &= - c \nabla \times \mathbf{E} \\ &= \nabla \times (\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B}) - \frac{c}{4 \pi} \nabla \times (\eta \nabla \times \mathbf{B}) . \end{aligned} \tag{23.17}\] If \(\eta\) is constant, then \[ \nabla \times (\nabla \times \mathbf{B}) = \nabla (\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B}) - \nabla^2 \mathbf{B}, \tag{23.18}\] so we find \[ \boxed{\frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t} = \nabla \times (\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B}) + \frac{c \eta}{4 \pi} \nabla^2 \mathbf{B}.} \tag{23.19}\] This is the last equation. The full set of MHD equation is \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\mathrm{d} \rho}{\mathrm{d} t} &= - \rho \nabla \cdot \mathbf{v}, \\ \rho \frac{\mathrm{d} \mathbf{v}}{\mathrm{d} t} &= \frac{1}{4 \pi} (\nabla \times \mathbf{B}) \times \mathbf{B}- \nabla p - \rho \nabla \Psi, \\ \rho \frac{\mathrm{d} \varepsilon}{\mathrm{d} t} &= - p \nabla \cdot \mathbf{v}, \\ \frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t} &= \nabla \times (\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B}) + \frac{c \eta}{4 \pi} \nabla^2 \mathbf{B}. \end{aligned} \tag{23.20}\] They can be converted to the Eulerian frame with the usual substitution \[ \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d} t} \longrightarrow \frac{\partial}{\partial t} + (\mathbf{v} \cdot \nabla) . \tag{23.21}\] If \(\eta = 0\), these are known as the ideal-MHD equations. If \(\eta > 0\), these are called the resistive-MHD equations.

23.4 Conservation Laws

It is possible to rearrange the terms of the momentum equation to obtain an equation that shows the conservation of the linear momentum: \[ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} & \frac{\partial (\rho \mathbf{v})}{\partial t} \\ & \quad + \nabla \cdot \biggl[ \rho \mathbf{v} \otimes \mathbf{v}+ \Bigl( p + \frac{B^2}{8 \pi} \Bigr) \mathbf{I} \\ & \qquad\qquad - \frac{\mathbf{B} \otimes \mathbf{B}}{4 \pi} \biggr] =\mathbf{f}, \end{aligned}} \tag{23.22}\] where \(\mathbf{f}\) is some external force, e.g., gravity. The derivation of this equation is tedious, but straight forward. Here, we limit ourselves to discuss a couple of the consequences of this equation. The first is that the magnetic field does not contribute to the momentum \(\rho \mathbf{v}\), consistently with Assumption 23.1. This is not the case in relativistic MHD, in which the inertia of the electromagnetic field is also taken into account. Second, we see that the Lorentz force splits into two components: one that resembles an additional pressure term \(B^2 / 8 \pi\), plus a term proportional to \(\mathbf{B} \otimes \mathbf{B}\). We will discuss these terms in more detail in the next set of notes.

The energy conservation equation now takes into account both the gas energy and the energy in the electromagnetic field and can be obtained by combining the gas-dynamics energy equation with the Poynting theorem. Specifically, the equation describing the energy conservation for the gas is \[ \begin{aligned} & \frac{\partial e}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot [(e + p) \mathbf{v}] =\mathbf{f} \cdot \mathbf{v}+ (\mathbf{j} \times \mathbf{B}) \cdot \mathbf{v}, \\ & e = \rho \varepsilon + \tfrac{1}{2} \rho v^2 . \end{aligned} \tag{23.23}\] This is just the usual energy equation, with the inclusion of a new term representing the work done by the Lorentz force. The latter can be rewritten using Equation 23.16 as \[ (\mathbf{j} \times \mathbf{B}) \cdot \mathbf{v}= - (\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B}) \cdot \mathbf{j}= c\mathbf{E} \cdot \mathbf{j}. \tag{23.24}\] You might recognize this as being the energy sink term in the Poynting equation: \[ \frac{\partial u_{\mathrm{EM}}}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot \mathbf{S}= - c\mathbf{j} \cdot \mathbf{E}, \tag{23.25}\] where \(u_{\mathrm{EM}}\) is the electromagnetic field energy, which in our case is: \[ u_{\mathrm{EM}} = \frac{B^2 + E^2}{8 \pi} \approx \frac{B^2}{8 \pi} = u_{\mathrm{B}}, \tag{23.26}\] consistently with our assumption that the dynamics is nonrelativistic. The Poynting vector is, using again Equation 23.16: \[ \mathbf{S}= \frac{c}{4 \pi} (\mathbf{E} \times \mathbf{B}) = \frac{\mathbf{B} \times (\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B})}{4 \pi} . \tag{23.27}\] Combining Equation 23.23 and Equation 23.25, we obtain \[ \frac{\partial (e + u_{\mathrm{B}})}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot [(e + p) \mathbf{v}+\mathbf{S}] =\mathbf{f} \cdot \mathbf{v}. \tag{23.28}\] In full form: \[ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} & \partial_t \left[ \rho \left( \varepsilon + \tfrac{1}{2} v^2 \right) + \frac{B^2}{8 \pi} \right] \\ & \quad + \nabla \cdot \left[ (e + p) \mathbf{v}+ \frac{\mathbf{B} \times (\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B})}{4 \pi} \right] =\mathbf{f} \cdot \mathbf{v}. \end{aligned}} \tag{23.29}\] This equation highlights the fact that magnetic and fluid energies are not separately conserved, but their sum is. In other words, it is possible to convert gas kinetic energy into magnetic energy (dynamo effect) or magnetic energy into kinetic and internal energy (magnetic reconnection).

23.5 In-class worksheet

  1. Write the Maxwell equations in CGS units.

  2. Write Ampere’s law in the nonrelativistic limit. Use it to eliminate the current in the Lorentz force per unit volume experienced by a magnetized fluid: \[ \mathbf{f}=\mathbf{j} \times \mathbf{B}. \tag{23.30}\]

  3. Write the Ohm’s law for an electrically conducting, quasi-neutral fluid. Use it to eliminate the electric field in Faraday’s law.

23.6 Exercises

Exercise 23.1 (Angular momentum of the solar wind)  

  1. Starting from the equations of ideal-MHD, show that the angular momentum of plasma, \[ J = \int_V \mathbf{r} \times \rho \mathbf{v} \mathrm{d} V, \tag{23.31}\] subject to an external force per unit mass \(\mathbf{f}\), satisfies \[ \frac{\mathrm{d} J}{\mathrm{d} t} = \int_V \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{f} \mathrm{d} V - \oint_S \left[ (\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathrm{d} \mathbf{S}) (\mathbf{r} \times \rho \mathbf{v}) + \left( p + \tfrac{B^2}{8 \pi} \right) \mathbf{r} \times \mathrm{d} \mathbf{S}- \tfrac{1}{4 \pi} (\mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{B}) (\mathbf{B} \cdot \mathrm{d} \mathbf{S}) \right] . \tag{23.32}\] [Hint. Follow the steps of Exercise 6.1.]

  2. Assume that the magnetic field is stationary in a frame rotating with the angular velocity of the Sun. Show that if the solar wind is infinitely conducting, then (in cylindrical coordinates \(r, \phi, z\)) \[ B_{\phi} v_R = (v_{\phi} - \Omega R) B_R, \tag{23.33}\] where \(\Omega\) is the angular velocity of the Sun.

  3. Assuming that the angular torque \(\dot{J}\) across a cylinder of height \(2 R\) is constant with \(R\). Show that it can be written as \[ - \dot{J} = 2 \pi \int_{- R}^R \left( R \rho v_R v_{\phi} - R \frac{B_{\phi} B_R}{4 \pi} \right) R \mathrm{d} z. \tag{23.34}\] Assume that the expression in parenthesis is independent of \(z\), and that \(4 \pi \rho R^2 v_R\), the Solar wind flux through the cylinder, is a constant. Show that \[ - \frac{\dot{J}}{4 \pi \rho R^2 v_R} = Rv_{\phi} \left( 1 - \frac{B_R^2}{4 \pi \rho v_R^2} \right) + \frac{B_R^2}{4 \pi \rho v_R^2} \Omega R^2 . \tag{23.35}\]

  4. There is a radius, \(R_A\), called the Alfvén radius where the radial Alfvén speed \(v_A = B_R / \sqrt{4 \pi \rho}\) is equal to \(v_R\). Evaluate the angular momentum loss per unit mass at this radius and show that it is equal to \(\Omega R_A^2\).

  5. If \(R_A\) is 10 \(R_{\odot}\), approximately what fraction of its mass must the Sun lose to slow its rotation down? Take the moment of inertia of the Sun to be \(0.06 M_{\odot} R_{\odot}^2\). What fraction of its mass has the Sun lost in 5 billion years if the solar wind was steady and the number density at the Earth is \(10 \, \mathrm{cm}^{- 3}\) and the velocity is \(300 \, \mathrm{km} / \mathrm{s}\)?
