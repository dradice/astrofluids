8  Hydrostatic Equilibrium and the Virial Theorem

8.1 The virial theorem

We consider a self-gravitating cloud of gas in equilibrium, described by the momentum equation in conservation form (Equation 6.7) \[ \frac{\partial (\rho \mathbf{v})}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot [\rho \mathbf{v} \otimes \mathbf{v}+ p\mathbf{I}] = - \rho \nabla \Psi, \tag{8.1}\] where \(\Psi\) is the gravitational potential. We consider the scalar moment of inertia \[ I = \frac{1}{2} \int_V \rho (\mathbf{r}) r^2 \mathrm{d} V, \tag{8.2}\] where \(V\) is a volume that encloses the whole gas cloud. The time derivative of \(I\) is (using the continuity equation) \[ \begin{alignedat}{2} \frac{\mathrm{d} I}{\mathrm{d} t} &{}={} && \frac{1}{2} \int_V \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} r^2 \mathrm{d} V \\ &{}={} && - \frac{1}{2} \int_V \nabla \cdot (\rho \mathbf{v}) r^2 \mathrm{d} V \\ &{}={} && - \frac{1}{2} \int_V \nabla \cdot (\rho \mathbf{v}r^2) \mathrm{d} V \\ &&& + \frac{1}{2} \int_V \rho \mathbf{v} \cdot \nabla r^2 \mathrm{d} V. \end{alignedat} \tag{8.3}\] The total divergence term is equal to a boundary integral \[ \int_V \nabla \cdot (\rho \mathbf{v}r^2) \mathrm{d} V = \int_{\partial V} \rho \mathbf{v}r^2 \cdot \mathbf{n} \mathrm{d} A, \tag{8.4}\] but the latter vanishes since, by assumption, \(\rho \rightarrow 0\) at the boundary of \(V\). So we find \[ \frac{\mathrm{d} I}{\mathrm{d} t} = \frac{1}{2} \int_V \rho \mathbf{v} \cdot \nabla r^2 \mathrm{d} V = \int_V \rho \mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{r} \mathrm{d} V. \tag{8.5}\] Here, we have used the fact that \(\nabla r =\mathbf{r}/ r\).

The second time derivative of \(I\) is \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\mathrm{d}^2 I}{\mathrm{d} t^2} &= \int_V \frac{\partial (\rho \mathbf{v})}{\partial t} \cdot \mathbf{r} \mathrm{d} V \\ &= - \int_V \rho \nabla \Psi \cdot \mathbf{r} \mathrm{d} V \\ &\qquad - \int_V \nabla \cdot [\rho \mathbf{v} \otimes \mathbf{v}+ p\mathbf{I}] \cdot \mathbf{r} \mathrm{d} V \\ &= - \int_V \rho \nabla \Psi \cdot \mathbf{r} \mathrm{d} V \\ &\qquad - \int_V \nabla \cdot [\rho (\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{r}) \mathbf{v}+ p\mathbf{r}] \mathrm{d} V \\ &\qquad + \int_V [\rho \mathbf{v} \otimes \mathbf{v}+ p\mathbf{I}] : \nabla \mathbf{r} \mathrm{d} V, \end{aligned} \tag{8.6}\] where the notation \(\mathbf{A}: \mathbf{B}\) denotes the inner product between two tensors: \[ \mathbf{A}: \mathbf{B}= \sum_{i = 1}^3 \sum_{k = 1}^3 A_{i k} B_{k i} = \mathrm{Trace} \left[ \left(\begin{array}{ccc} A_{x x} & A_{x y} & A_{x z}\\ A_{y x} & A_{y y} & A_{y z}\\ A_{z x} & A_{z y} & A_{z z} \end{array}\right) \left(\begin{array}{ccc} B_{x x} & B_{x y} & B_{x z}\\ B_{y x} & B_{y y} & B_{y z}\\ B_{z x} & B_{z y} & B_{z z} \end{array}\right) \right] . \tag{8.7}\] Once again, we can drop the total divergence terms, since, by the divergence theorem, they are equal to boundary integrals that vanish because \(\rho\) and \(p\) vanish at the boundary of \(V\). We find \[ \frac{\mathrm{d}^2 I}{\mathrm{d} t^2} = - \int_V \rho \nabla \Psi \cdot \mathbf{r} \mathrm{d} V + \int_V [\rho \mathbf{v} \otimes \mathbf{v}+ p\mathbf{I}] : \nabla \mathbf{r} \mathrm{d} V. \tag{8.8}\] The second term in the RHS is simpler than it looks, because \[ \nabla \mathbf{r}= \left(\begin{array}{ccc} \partial_x x & \partial_x y & \partial_x z\\ \partial_y x & \partial_y y & \partial_y z\\ \partial_z x & \partial_z y & \partial_z z \end{array}\right) = \left(\begin{array}{ccc} 1 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & 1 & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{array}\right) =\mathbf{I}. \tag{8.9}\] So everything simplifies to \[ \int_V [\rho \mathbf{v} \otimes \mathbf{v}+ p\mathbf{I}] : \nabla \mathbf{r} \mathrm{d} V = \int_V \rho v^2 \mathrm{d} V + 3 \int_V p \mathrm{d} V =: 2 K + 3 \Pi, \tag{8.10}\] where \(K\) is the kinetic energy of the gas and \(\Pi\) is the integrated pressure.

The first term in the RHS of Equation 8.8 requires a little more work. First, we need to compute an expression for the gravitational potential in terms of the mass density \(\rho\). To do this, we remember that the gravitational potential generated by a distribution of point masses with mass \(m_i\) at locations \(\mathbf{r}_i\) is given by \[ \Psi (\mathbf{r}) = - \sum_{i = 1}^N \frac{Gm_i}{ | \mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_i |} . \tag{8.11}\] This can be generalized to the case of a mass density distribution by converting the sum into an integral \[ \Psi (\mathbf{r}) = - \int_V \frac{G \rho (\mathbf{r}')}{ | \mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}' |} \mathrm{d}^3 r' . \tag{8.12}\] Inserting this into the first term in the RHS of Equation 8.8 yields \[ \int_V \rho \nabla \Psi \cdot \mathbf{r} \mathrm{d}^3 r = - \int_V \mathrm{d}^3 r \rho (\mathbf{r}) \nabla_{\mathbf{r}} \int_V \mathrm{d}^3 r' \frac{G \rho (\mathbf{r}')}{ | \mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}' |} \cdot \mathbf{r}, \tag{8.13}\] where we have used the notation \(\nabla_{\mathbf{r}}\) to remind ourselves that the derivatives are computed with respect to the unprimed position \(\mathbf{r}\). In particular, note that \(\rho (\mathbf{r}')\) in the integral is not affected by the derivative, since it is a function of \(\mathbf{r}'\) and not of \(\mathbf{r}\). The only term that we need to differentiate is the denominator: \[ \begin{aligned} & \nabla_{\mathbf{r}} \frac{1}{| \mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}' |} \\ & \quad = \nabla_{\mathbf{r}} \frac{1}{\sqrt{(x - x')^2 + (y - y')^2 + (z - z')^2}} \\ & \quad = \frac{- 1}{[(x - x')^2 + (y - y')^2 + (z - z')^2]^{3 / 2}} \\ & \qquad\quad \times \left(\begin{array}{c} x - x' \\ y - y' \\ z - z' \end{array}\right) \\ & \quad = - \frac{\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'}{| \mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}' |^3} . \end{aligned} \tag{8.14}\] Inserting the last expression into Equation 8.13, we obtain \[ \begin{aligned} & \int_V \rho \nabla \Psi \cdot \mathbf{r} \mathrm{d}^3 r \\ & \quad = \int_V \mathrm{d}^3 r \int_V \mathrm{d}^3 r' \frac{G \rho (\mathbf{r}') \rho (\mathbf{r}) }{ | \mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}' |^3} (\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}') \cdot \mathbf{r} \\ & \quad = \int_V \mathrm{d}^3 r \int_V \mathrm{d}^3 r' \frac{G \rho (\mathbf{r}') \rho (\mathbf{r}) }{ | \mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}' |^3} | \mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}' |^2 \\ & \qquad + \int_V \mathrm{d}^3 r \int_V \mathrm{d}^3 r' \frac{G \rho (\mathbf{r}') \rho (\mathbf{r}) }{ | \mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}' |^3} (\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}') \cdot \mathbf{r}' \\ & \quad = \int_V \mathrm{d}^3 r \int_V \mathrm{d}^3 r' \frac{G \rho (\mathbf{r}') \rho (\mathbf{r}) }{| \mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}' |} \\ & \qquad + \int_V \mathrm{d}^3 r' \rho (\mathbf{r}') \mathbf{r}' \cdot \int_V \mathrm{d}^3 r \frac{G \rho (\mathbf{r}) (\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}') }{ | \mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}' |^3} \\ & \quad = - \int_V \mathrm{d}^3 r \Psi (\mathbf{r}) \rho (\mathbf{r}) \\ & \qquad - \int_V \rho (\mathbf{r}') \nabla_{\mathbf{r}'} \Psi (\mathbf{r}') \cdot \mathbf{r}' \mathrm{d}^3 r' . \end{aligned} \tag{8.15}\] We have used the fact that (note the difference in sign compared to Equation 8.14) \[ \nabla_{\mathbf{r}'} \frac{1}{| \mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}' |} = \frac{\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'}{| \mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}' |^3} . \tag{8.16}\] The last term on the RHS of Equation 8.15 is identical to the LHS, so we find \[ - \int_V \rho \nabla \Psi \cdot \mathbf{r} \mathrm{d}^3 r= \frac{1}{2} \int_V \mathrm{d}^3 r \Psi (\mathbf{r}) \rho (\mathbf{r}) = W, \tag{8.17}\] \(W\) being the gravitational potential energy.

For a stationary configuration \(\mathrm{d}^2 I / \mathrm{d} t^2 = 0\), so Equation 8.8 yields the virial theorem: \[ \boxed{W + 2 K + 3 \Pi = 0.} \tag{8.18}\] For an ideal gas \(p = (\Gamma - 1) \rho \varepsilon\) (Equation 2.15), so \[ \Pi = \int_V p \mathrm{d} V = (\Gamma - 1) \int_V \rho \varepsilon \mathrm{d} V = (\Gamma - 1) U, \tag{8.19}\] where \(U\) is the total internal energy. In this case, the virial theorem reads \[ \boxed{W + 2 K + 3 (\Gamma - 1) U = 0.} \tag{8.20}\]

8.2 Hydrostatic equilibria

A fluid is in hydrostatic equilibrium when \(\mathbf{v} = 0\), so that Euler’s equation reduces to \(\nabla p = - \rho \nabla \Psi\). For a spherical star, this reads \(\mathrm{d} p / \mathrm{d} r = - G M (r) \rho / r^2\), where \(M (r)\) is the mass enclosed within the radius \(r\) (see also Equation 4.21).

The virial theorem has an important consequence for the stability of hydrostatic equilibria. Consider a cloud of gas or star in hydrostatic equilibrium, that is with no large scale motion (no rotation). Then the virial theorem states that \[ W + 3 (\Gamma - 1) U = 0. \tag{8.21}\] On the other hand, the energy of the cloud is \[ E = W + U. \tag{8.22}\] The energy must be negative for the system to be bound, \(E \leq 0\). This implies \[ U \leq - W = 3 (\Gamma - 1) U \Longrightarrow [- 1 + 3 (\Gamma - 1)] U \geq 0, \tag{8.23}\] since \(U \geq 0\), this requires \[ \Gamma \geq 4 / 3. \tag{8.24}\] Monoatomic gas has \(\Gamma = 5 / 3\), so regular stars are stable. Matter dominated by relativistic particles, such as radiation or the relativistic degenerate electrons in white dwarfs and in the iron cores of massive stars, has \(\Gamma \approx 4 / 3\). Stars made of such matter are only marginally stable, and small effects that reduce \(\Gamma\) below \(4/3\) can make them collapse. This happens in the iron cores of massive stars, where electron captures reduce the pressure, triggering core-collapse supernovae (Chapter 3 and Section 4.3), and in very massive stars, where the production of electron–positron pairs triggers so-called pair-instability supernovae.

A surprising consequence of the virial theorem is that self-gravitating systems have negative specific heat. Consider a self-gravitating cloud (a protostar) emitting infrared radiation. As its energy \(E\) becomes more negative its gravitational binding energy \(- W\) must increase, because \(U \geq 0\). We can use Equation 8.21 and Equation 8.22 to solve for \(U\) in terms of \(E\): \[ U = - \frac{E}{3 \Gamma - 4} . \tag{8.25}\] If \(\Gamma > 4 / 3\), Equation 8.25 predicts that \(U\) will increase as \(E\) becomes more negative. Meaning that the system heats up as it loses energy! Eventually the cloud contracts to the point that nuclear fusion starts occurring and a star is born. Nuclear burning prevents the star from heating up further.

TipExample

Could the Sun be powered by gravitational contraction alone? In this case, the Sun would radiate its gravitational binding energy, \(| W | \sim G M_{\odot}^2 / R_{\odot}\), over a time \[ t_{\mathrm{KH}} \sim \frac{G M_{\odot}^2}{R_{\odot} L_{\odot}} \approx 3 \times 10^7\,\mathrm{yr}, \tag{8.26}\] where \(L_{\odot} \approx 3.8 \times 10^{26}\,\mathrm{W}\) (\(3.8 \times 10^{33}\,\mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}}\)) is the luminosity of the Sun. This is the so-called Kelvin–Helmholtz time. It is much shorter than the age of the Earth, about \(4.5 \times 10^9\) years, so the Sun must have another source of energy: nuclear fusion.

TipExample

The hot gas in a galaxy cluster is in approximate virial equilibrium in the gravitational potential of the cluster. Consider a cluster with mass \(M \sim 10^{15}\,M_{\odot}\) and radius \(R \sim 1\,\mathrm{Mpc}\), and assume, for simplicity, that the gas has the same distribution as the total mass. For a uniform sphere \(W = - \frac{3}{5} G M^2 / R\), while \(3 \Pi = 3 M k_B T / (\mu m_u)\), so the virial theorem gives \[ k_B T \sim \frac{G M \mu m_u}{5 R} \approx 5\,\mathrm{keV}, \tag{8.27}\] or \(T \sim 6 \times 10^7\,\mathrm{K}\), where we have taken \(\mu \approx 0.6\) for fully ionized gas. This is why galaxy clusters are bright X-ray sources, as in the case of the Bullet cluster (Figure 1.1).

8.3 Application to star formation

Consider a spherical cloud of radius \(R\). For simplicity, we take its density \(\bar{\rho}\) to be uniform. Then the gravitational potential energy is \[ - W = G \int_0^R 4 \pi r \rho (r) M (r) \mathrm{d} r = \frac{16 \pi^2}{15} G \bar{\rho}^2 R^5 = \frac{3}{5} \frac{G M^2}{R}, \tag{8.28}\] where \(M = \frac{4 \pi}{3} \bar{\rho} R^3\) is the mass of the cloud and \[ M (r) = \int_0^r 4 \pi r'^2 \rho (r') \mathrm{d} r' \tag{8.29}\] is the mass enclosed within the radius \(r\). For an ideal gas, the pressure is given by \[ p = \frac{k_B T}{\mu m_u} \rho, \tag{8.30}\] where \(\mu\) is the mean molecular weight (Equation 1.6), \(m_u\) is the atomic mass unit, \(k_B\) is the Boltzmann constant and \(T\) is the temperature. We can assume \(T\) to be constant. This is a good approximation for optically thin clouds which can efficiently cool by emitting radiation. With this approximation \[ \Pi = \int_0^R 4 \pi r^2 p (r) \mathrm{d} r = \frac{4}{3} \pi c_s^2 \bar{\rho} R^3, \tag{8.31}\] where \[ c_s^2 = \frac{k_B T}{\mu m_u} \tag{8.32}\] is a constant. As we will see later, \(c_s\) turns out to be the sound speed of the gas (Chapter 9). The condition for collapse of the cloud is that \(3 \Pi < | W |\), which implies that collapse will occur for a cloud with \[ R > R_J, \qquad R_J^2 = \frac{15 c_s^2}{4 \pi G \bar{\rho}} . \tag{8.33}\] \(R_J\) is called the Jeans length. Typical molecular clouds have \(R \gg R_J\), so instead of collapsing to an object with mass equal to the mass of the cloud, they fragment into smaller pieces. The mass of these fragments can be estimated as the mass contained within one Jeans length, the so-called Jeans mass1: \[ \begin{aligned} M_J &= \frac{4 \pi}{3} \bar{\rho} R_J^3 = \frac{15^{3 / 2} c_s^3}{3 (4 \pi \bar{\rho})^{1 / 2} G^{3 / 2}} \\ &= \frac{15^{3 / 2} k_B^{3 / 2}}{3 (4 \pi)^{1 / 2} m_u^2 G^{3 / 2}} \frac{T^{3 / 2}}{\mu^2 n^{1 / 2}} . \end{aligned} \tag{8.34}\] Inserting the values of the constants, we find \[ M_J \approx 94\,M_{\odot} \left( \frac{T}{\mathrm{K}} \right)^{3 / 2} \left( \frac{\mathrm{cm}^{- 3}}{n} \right)^{1 / 2} \frac{1}{\mu^2} . \tag{8.35}\] For a typical molecular cloud with \(T = 10\,\mathrm{K}\), \(n = 100\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}\), and \(\mu = 2\) (for molecular hydrogen) the Jeans length is \(R_J \approx 1.5\,\mathrm{pc}\) and the Jeans mass is \(M_J \approx 70\,M_{\odot}\). This is much larger than the mass of a typical star. Note however that the Jeans mass decreases with increasing \(n\). So, as long as the gas can efficiently cool and remain at low temperature, the fragments collapse and undergo further fragmentation. In the dense cores of molecular clouds, with \(n \sim 10^4\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}\), the Jeans mass is \(M_J \approx 7\,M_{\odot}\), and at even higher densities it approaches the mass of typical stars.

While the arguments we have given here give an order of magnitude estimate for the mass of protostars, star formation is strongly influenced by a number of factors that we have neglected. These include turbulence and magnetic fields. Moreover, star formation strongly depends on the ability of the gas to cool to low temperature, as shown by Equation 8.35.

8.4 Tidal disruption

A star in hydrostatic equilibrium is held together by its own gravity. However, if the star passes close to a massive black hole (BH), such as those found in the centers of galaxies, the gravitational field of the BH can tear it apart. This is called a tidal disruption event (TDE).

Consider a star of mass \(M_*\) and radius \(R_*\) at a distance \(r\) from a BH of mass \(M_{\mathrm{BH}}\) (Figure 8.1). The BH pulls more strongly on the side of the star facing it than on the opposite side. The difference between the two accelerations, the so-called tidal acceleration, is \[ \frac{G M_{\mathrm{BH}}}{(r - R_*)^2} - \frac{G M_{\mathrm{BH}}}{(r + R_*)^2} \simeq \frac{4 G M_{\mathrm{BH}} R_*}{r^3}, \tag{8.36}\] where we have Taylor expanded for \(R_* \ll r\). On the other hand, the self-gravity of the star pulls the two sides towards each other with a relative acceleration \(2 G M_* / R_*^2\). So the star is disrupted if \[ \frac{4 G M_{\mathrm{BH}} R_*}{r^3} > \frac{2 G M_*}{R_*^2}, \tag{8.37}\] that is if \[ \boxed{r < R_T = \eta \left( \frac{M_{\mathrm{BH}}}{M_*} \right)^{1 / 3} R_*,} \tag{8.38}\] where \(\eta\) is a factor of order unity (\(\eta = 2^{1 / 3}\) in our simple estimate). \(R_T\) is called the tidal radius.

Note that \(R_T\) grows only like \(M_{\mathrm{BH}}^{1 / 3}\), while the size of the BH grows like \(M_{\mathrm{BH}}\). A star orbiting a nonrotating BH cannot have stable circular orbits closer than the innermost stable circular orbit (ISCO), \[ r_{\mathrm{ISCO}} = \frac{6 G M_{\mathrm{BH}}}{c^2} \approx 9\,\mathrm{km} \left( \frac{M_{\mathrm{BH}}}{M_{\odot}} \right) . \tag{8.39}\] If \(R_T < r_{\mathrm{ISCO}}\), the star plunges into the BH before it is disrupted, and it is swallowed whole. Setting \(\eta = 1\), the condition \(R_T > r_{\mathrm{ISCO}}\) gives \[ \begin{aligned} & \left( \frac{M_{\mathrm{BH}}}{M_*} \right)^{1 / 3} R_* > \frac{6 G M_{\mathrm{BH}}}{c^2} \\ \Longrightarrow \quad & M_{\mathrm{BH}}^{2 / 3} < \frac{c^2}{6 G} \frac{R_*}{M_*^{1 / 3}}, \end{aligned} \tag{8.40}\] or \[ \boxed{M_{\mathrm{BH}} < \frac{c^3 R_*^{3 / 2}}{6^{3 / 2} G^{3 / 2} M_*^{1 / 2}}} \approx 2 \times 10^7\,M_{\odot}, \tag{8.41}\] for a Sun-like star, with \(R_* = R_{\odot} = 7 \times 10^8\,\mathrm{m}\) and \(M_* = M_{\odot} = 2 \times 10^{30}\,\mathrm{kg}\). In other words, only BHs lighter than about \(10^7\,M_{\odot}\) can disrupt Sun-like stars. Note that the spin of the BH changes this estimate, because the ISCO of a rotating BH can be closer to (or farther from) the BH. Even if we only require the star to be disrupted outside the horizon of the BH, \(r_{\mathrm{BH}} = 2 G M_{\mathrm{BH}} / c^2\), we find \(M_{\mathrm{BH}} \lesssim 10^8\,M_{\odot}\).

8.5 In-class worksheet

For a magnetic star the virial theorem becomes: \[ W + 2 K + 3 \Pi +\mathfrak{M}= 0, \tag{8.42}\] where the new term is the magnetic energy of the star, which is \[ \mathfrak{M}= \frac{1}{2 \mu_0} \int B^2 \mathrm{d} V . \tag{8.43}\]

  1. Find the condition for stability of a nonrotating star in terms of \(\Gamma\) and \(\mathfrak{M}\).

  2. What is the maximum magnetic field strength for a Sun-like star (\(R = 7 \times 10^5\,\mathrm{km}\), \(M = 2 \times 10^{30}\,\mathrm{kg}\))? Neglect factors of order unity, and give the answer in tesla and in gauss (\(1\,\mathrm{T} = 10^4\,\mathrm{G}\)).

  3. What is the maximum magnetic field strength for a neutron star (\(R = 12\,\mathrm{km}\), \(M = 3 \times 10^{30}\,\mathrm{kg}\))?

8.6 Exercises

Exercise 8.1 (Self-gravitating slab) An infinite, static slab of incompressible fluid with uniform density \(\rho_0\) fills the region \(| z | < a\) and is held together by its own gravity.

  1. Compute the gravitational acceleration inside and outside the slab, and the pressure profile inside it. Use Poisson’s equation, \(\nabla^2 \Psi = 4 \pi G \rho\).

  2. Model the disk of a galaxy as such a slab, with \(\rho_0 = 10^{-23}\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}} = 10^{-20}\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\) and \(a = 10^{19}\,\mathrm{m}\). A star starts at rest at \(z = a\). How fast does it cross the mid-plane, and what is the period of its vertical oscillation?

Adapted from Clarke and Carswell (2007), Problem 8.

Exercise 8.2 (Mass of a molecular cloud) A self-gravitating cloud of molecular hydrogen \(\left( p = nk_B T = \frac{2}{5} \rho \varepsilon \right)\) is measured to have radius \(R = 0.1\,\mathrm{pc}\) and temperature \(T = 10\,\mathrm{K}\). Estimate the mass of the cloud.

Exercise 8.3 (Temperature of the solar core) Estimate the temperature at the center of the Sun, assuming that density and pressure change over a length scale comparable to the solar radius. Make the estimate twice: first assuming that the Sun is supported by gas pressure, then assuming that it is supported by radiation pressure. Which assumption is more plausible, and why?

Adapted from Clarke and Carswell (2007), Problem 11.

Exercise 8.4 (Numerical solution of the Lane–Emden equation) The Lane–Emden equation describes the structure of a star with a polytropic equation of state, \(p = K \rho^{1 + 1 / n}\). It reads \[ \frac{1}{\xi^2} \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d} \xi} \left( \xi^2 \frac{\mathrm{d} \theta}{\mathrm{d} \xi} \right) = - \theta^n, \qquad \theta (0) = 1, \qquad \theta' (0) = 0, \tag{8.44}\] where \(\theta^n\) is the density in units of the central density, and \(\xi\) is the radius in suitable units. Write a code to compute numerically the solution of the Lane–Emden equation for a given \(n\).

  1. Validate your code by comparing numerical and analytic solution for \(n = 0, 1, 5\).

  2. Plot the solution for \(n = 1.5\) and find the radius of the polytrope, i.e., the value \(\xi_r\) at which \(\theta (\xi_r) = 0\).

Hint: use the solve_ivp function from scipy.integrate.
